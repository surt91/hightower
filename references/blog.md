# Blog Post Outline: "Routing Like It's 1969 — Hightower's Line-Search Algorithm in Rust"

## 1. Hook: a modern problem, a 1969 solution

- Motivating scenario (the actual one): drawing orthogonal edges in a diagram /
  graph layout — connect two boxes with a clean, right-angled line that dodges
  all the other boxes. Interactive, so it must be *fast*; it should look
  *straight and calm* (few bends); it does **not** have to be the shortest path.
- Tease: the algorithm that fits this profile was published in 1969 at Bell Labs,
  written in FORTRAN II for the IBM 7094 — for routing printed circuit boards.
- **Image 1 (hero):** side-by-side of the same obstacle scene solved two ways —
  left: Lee-style BFS flood fill with the whole grid colored by wavefront
  (hundreds of visited cells), right: Hightower's solution with just a handful
  of probe lines and the final red path. Caption: "same problem, three orders of
  magnitude less work."

## 2. The problem, and why "shortest" is the wrong goal

- Formal-ish statement: rectilinear path between two points among axis-parallel
  segment obstacles (rectangles = 4 segments).
- Why not A*/Lee on a grid: you must pick a grid resolution; memory and time
  scale with *area*, not with *clutter*. Quote the paper's own example: a 9×9
  inch board at 1-mil resolution = **81 million cells** — in 1969, on a machine
  with kilowords of memory.
- Why not shortest: for diagrams (and much PCB wiring) you want few bends and
  fast answers; optimality is negotiable. This trade is the whole design point.
- **Image 2:** small diagram-routing example: two boxes connected by (a) a
  shortest-but-ugly staircase path vs (b) Hightower's straighter path with 2
  bends. Caption: "shortest ≠ prettiest."

## 3. Historical interlude: line search vs. flood fill

- One paragraph on Lee (1961): guaranteed shortest, complete, but grid-bound —
  the "breadth-first search" of routing.
- Hightower (1969): works on the *continuous plane*, stores only line segments;
  one of the classic "line-probe" algorithms (mention the sibling: Mikami–Tabuchi
  1968).
- Fun artifacts from the paper to reproduce: it solved the **Hampton Court
  hedge maze** in 0.0005 hours, and routed real two-sided PCBs. The paper's
  plots were drawn on a dataplotter.
- **Image 3:** photo-style reproduction: my implementation solving a
  Hampton-Court-like maze, styled like the paper's page-19 plot (black walls,
  thick path). Ideally side by side with a scan of the original figure.

## 4. The algorithm, part 1: covers and escape lines

- Introduce the two key concepts with minimal jargon:
  - **Cover**: the nearest obstacle segment that blocks you in each of the four
    directions (the "ceiling", "floor", and two "walls" of the point).
  - **Escape line**: the maximal horizontal/vertical segment through a point,
    fenced in by its covers.
- Key mental model: *a point's escape lines are everything it can see; the
  algorithm is about extending sight around corners.*
- **Image 4:** one point in a cluttered scene; its four covers highlighted and
  labeled, everything else dimmed. Second panel: its two escape lines drawn in,
  stopping one unit short of the covers.
- **Image 5 (optional, small):** the paper's "cover (verb)" definition as a
  redraw of its Figure 3: which segments cover point p and which don't.

## 5. The algorithm, part 2: escape points — sneaking around corners

- **Process I ("slip around the end"):** if your horizontal escape line pokes out
  past the end of the ceiling that blocks you, step one unit past that end —
  from there, a new vertical line escapes upward. That step-around point is an
  *escape point*; the new line is added to the network.
- **Process II ("retreat along the wall"):** when no end can be reached (you're
  inside a box), walk back along your own escape line, unit by unit, trying to
  branch off perpendicular lines until one fits — this is how it crawls out of
  concave pockets.
- Rule that makes it terminate (and fail!): **an escape line is never used
  twice.**
- **Image 6 (3-panel or GIF):** Process I step by step: (1) point with blocking
  cover, (2) candidate point one unit past the cover's end, (3) new vertical
  escape line reaching past the cover.
- **Image 7:** Process II inside an open box: the retreat positions ticking back
  toward the point, then the successful perpendicular escape through the mouth.

## 6. The algorithm, part 3: two networks race toward each other

- Bidirectional growth: alternate between a network rooted at start `s` and one
  at target `t`; each turn adds one escape line. The moment a line from one
  network crosses a perpendicular line of the other → a path exists.
- Path reconstruction: every line remembers the escape point it grew from —
  walk both parent chains from the crossing point; the concatenation is
  automatically rectilinear.
- **Image 8 (the centerpiece — animation/GIF or interactive):** frame-by-frame
  growth on a medium scene: blue lines for the `s`-network, green for the
  `t`-network, dots for escape points, orange circle at the moment of
  intersection, then the red reconstructed path fading in. (Direct SVG frames
  from `examples/animate.rs`; colors deliberately match my original whiteboard
  sketches.) If the blog supports it: a WASM widget where the reader drags
  obstacles and watches the route update live.

## 7. Making it pretty: path refinement

- The raw escape-point chain overshoots and zigzags; show it honestly.
- Cleanup: collapse collinear runs; then the paper's "second improvement":
  extend each path segment as far as obstacles allow — if the extension hits a
  later path segment, cut out everything in between.
- **Image 9 (before/after slider or two panels):** raw path (thin, wobbly, with
  overshoots) vs refined path (bold, 3 bends). Annotate one removed detour.

## 8. Implementation notes (Rust)

- Design decisions worth a paragraph each:
  - Integer coordinates (`i64`), exact intersection tests, no floats, no grid —
    the entire state is a few `Vec`s of segments.
  - Covers via `BTreeMap<coord, spans>` + range scan (the bisection idea from my
    original notes).
  - Every step emits a `TraceEvent`; the SVG renderer replays them — *all
    figures in this post are outputs of the algorithm itself.*

## 9. Where it breaks: not shortest, not even complete

- Be upfront — two distinct failure modes:
  1. Paths can be longer than optimal (show one).
  2. It can *miss existing paths entirely*: since escape lines are never reused,
     a box entered through a narrow mouth may be unescapable for the algorithm.
     The paper admits this; in his experiments a path was always found when one
     existed — my property tests found counterexamples, show a minimal one
     (or, if none is found, discuss why they're rare).
- Consequence for practice: treat it as a fast heuristic with a fallback
  (A* on demand) — exactly the plan from my original design notes.
- **Image 11:** the narrow-mouth failure scene: obstacles, the stuck network's
  lines, and (ghosted in gray) the path a BFS would have found. Caption: "the
  algorithm's one blind spot."

## 10. How fast is it, actually?

- Micro-benchmark vs. a naive grid BFS (Lee-style) on the same scenes; vary
  (a) board area at fixed clutter, (b) clutter at fixed area.
- Expected story: Hightower ~independent of area, scales with number of
  segments; BFS scales with area. Include absolute numbers (ns–µs range) to
  make the "interactive diagram editing" case.
- **Image 12:** log–log chart, two lines (Hightower vs grid BFS) over board
  side length; second small chart over obstacle count. (Generate with the
  standard plotting setup; keep the post's color scheme.)
- Honesty note: comparison is vs *my* naive BFS, not a tuned router.

## 11. Closing

- What I'd add next: multiple escape points + backtracking (making it complete-ish
  — the extension I sketched in my notes), routing multiple nets with
  already-routed paths as new obstacles (the paper's four "modes"), Mikami–Tabuchi
  as the complete line-probe alternative.
- The takeaway: a 55-year-old memory-starved algorithm is a perfect fit for a
  2020s interactive UI problem — constraints age better than hardware.
- Links: original paper (DOI 10.1145/800260.809014), repo, Lee's 1961 paper,
  Mikami–Tabuchi.
